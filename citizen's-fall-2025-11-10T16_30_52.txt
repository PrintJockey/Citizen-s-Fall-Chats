CHAPTER 1

PROLOGUE

“So, this is it... this is how it all ends,” he thought as he slammed into the dirt, rocks and branches gouging him from every angle. 
His head spun, a nauseating whirl from the fall, he was sprawled legs and arms out wide, eyes squeezed shut against the chaos. He could hear the van’s engine fading into the distance, tires crunching over the gravel road. With trembling hands, he scrambled to rip the hood from his head, gulping air as he blinked into the dim light. 
He seized a final glimpse of the van speeding away, dust settling over the road’s edge where it cut through the jagged spine of the Northwestern Range—or what remained after the Reclamation.
#
How did it end up like this? All he’d tried to do was the right thing—play by the rules and get ahead just like anyone else—but they’d hounded him until he had nothing left and had no option but to push back.
He had no idea where he was; they’d been driving for hours, or so it felt. Time was a funny thing when you had a hood over your head and your arms and legs are shackled to a van floor, with only the rumble of the tires on the road for company.
He’d been expecting something like this to happen; he just wasn’t sure what it would be. Jail wasn’t going to be an option—he was too well-known now. Infamous would be more accurate, and it would certainly get out that he was in custody. Which would more than likely provide a location for the protest movement to picket against, just like they did at the courthouse during his so-called trial. He hadn’t been at the courthouse personally, but the crowd had caused more than enough disruption and embarrassment for the government to avoid giving his supporters a new focus.
No, they simply couldn’t risk him becoming more famous. They had to take the steam out of the protest movement that had been threatening to build into something that couldn’t be stopped easily.
Was it going to be a bullet in a back room? Was it going to be torture and public humiliation, or something even worse? Not knowing his fate past the next few hours of survival had made the last few months a living hell. If he stopped running and got caught, the consequences were as dire as they could be.
Today, that day had come.
Somehow they’d found him. He wasn’t sure how—was it one of the cameras on every street corner? Was it a dutiful citizen noticing him and reporting it, earning themselves some social cohesion points? It didn’t matter anymore; they’d caught up with him. When it came to it, there was no heroic standoff or fight against the odds. No, it was quiet, efficient, and a total surprise.
He’d been skulking through the rusted sprawl of the Mid-Continental Belt, where factories choked the plains from old Kansas to the Texan fringe, hiding out among the workers for a month.
He’d managed to steal a high-vis uniform from one of the big companies in the area and could walk around without anyone really noticing—he was just another worker. He’d worn a hard hat and safety glasses to make himself less recognisable; not that many people remebered what he looked like anyway.
He’d even been able to blend into the throng of uniformed workers going into the factories with the morning shift. From there, it was easy to get more uniform pieces, have a shower, and even visit the company canteen twice a day. It was, he thought, the perfect deception—become just another body in a nameless crowd.
He was feeling almost comfortable for the first time in months when the van screeched to a halt next to him as he walked to his bolt hole in an abandoned office building for the night. The door to the van slid open quickly and violently. Two sets of hands snatched him off his feet and bundled him backward inside, while another set thrust a hood over his head. There was no struggle—the arms attached to those hands were far too strong and practiced. It took no more than five seconds, and he was off the street as the van sped away.
Almost immediately, a female voice began addressing him coldly. He tried to calm himself down so he could hear the words she was saying instead of the blood pounding in his ears.
“Mr. Dalton, you have been convicted, in absentia, of crimes against the state that include, but are not limited to, seditious libel and seditious conspiracy. For these and other crimes, you have had your citizenship revoked. You no longer have any rights. This decision cannot be appealed and is permanent—not even an act of government can reverse it.”
The voice softened slightly. “Mr. Dalton, this means you can no longer exist in our society. You are not welcome anywhere under the jurisdiction of the Bi-Continental Government of the Americas. To be found inside the limits of our borders will result in immediate and summary execution.”
With finality, the voice said, “Mr. Dalton, by the power invested in me as an authorised person, I hereby consider this judgment to have been delivered. Driver, take him away.”
With that the van jerked to a halt and a door slid open. Someone got out—he guessed it was the owner of the female voice.
#
The hours since being snatched off the street had raced by in a blur of monotony punctuated by pain, maybe six or seven by his best guess.
He had tried counting the time in seconds and minutes, but when the van left the road and started on what felt like a goat track, he lost focus. It was hard to keep a regular cadence when every bump smashed like a punch to the kidneys as his spine compressed against the steel bench he was restrained to. His shoulders were stretched tight, his hands forced painfully behind him in cuffs.
The guards—or whatever they were—hadn’t uttered a word since grabbing him off the street and bundling him into the van.
Every time he asked even a simple question, like who they were or where they were taking him, the answer had been a sharp poke in the ribs with one of their batons—or, like the last time, a swift crack across the head that left him seeing stars.
Admittedly, asking them if they were okay and whether they needed to stop for a break might have been provocative.
Eventually, they stopped, but he didn’t have more than a few seconds to enjoy the blissful lack of movement. No sooner had they halted than he was unlocked from the floor and dragged out of the van.
The shackles came off his ankles and legs; the hood stayed on.
The two guards took an arm each, half-lifting, half-marching him to what felt like the side of a road—then unceremoniously shoved him.
That was when he realized he wasn’t on flat ground. Where he expected to hit solid earth, there wasn’t any. 
He struck a steep slope and cartwheeled—one, two, three, four times—arms and legs flailing. Crashing into the foliage, gravity won.
The spinning in his head slowly wound down and In the quiet aftermath of his violent exit from the van, he gauged his situation
He’d been shoved off the side of the road down a steep embankment and had ended up at the edge of a forest, buried in some bushes.
He gauged his condition: What hurt? Did he have any serious injuries?
No, he didn’t think so. Apart from the pain of bouncing down the slope and being winded somewhere after the second or third barrel roll, he was relatively unscathed.
However, he had nothing on him. He was dressed in the same stolen work clothes he’d been wearing when they dragged him off the street—at least it was relatively new gear, stolen from the laundry a few days ago—but they had taken his boots. 
That was about the extent of his inventory: no phone to call family (not that anyone would answer anyway), no wallet, money, or ID, no food, water, not even a jacket.
He gingerly stood up, making sure his initial assessment of his lack of injuries was accurate. Yes, all good so far—though he was pretty sure he’d hurt more later.
The forest behind him was black and impenetrable, swallowing the fading light of the day. Snow gleamed on the peaks hemming the valley—somewhere deep in the Cascades or Rockies, far from the Belt’s smog. They’d hauled him north, alright, into the wild, lawless territory that no country claimed or controlled.
The van was long gone, the dust already settling from its speedy exit. The noise of it driving away was being replaced by an oppressive silence. Having lived in the city all his life, he didn’t like the silence too much, and the silence here was far too loud.
It didn’t seem like it had snowed down here recently. Thankfully, he was a few hundred meters below the snow line, but the sun would be going down soon, and the cold was already biting.
